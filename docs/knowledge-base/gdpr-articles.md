# GDPR Key Articles — Knowledge Base

This document contains the most compliance-relevant articles from the General Data Protection
Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2016/679). These articles are the primary reference for assessing
whether data processing activities are lawful.

---

## Article 4 — Definitions

**Article 4(1) — Personal Data**
"Personal data" means any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person
("data subject"). An identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or
indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier such as a name, an identification number,
location data, an online identifier or to one or more factors specific to the physical,
physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural or social identity of that natural person.

Key point: IP addresses, cookie identifiers, device fingerprints, and email addresses are all
personal data under this definition. You do not need to know someone's name — if any combination
of data can single out an individual, it is personal data.

**Article 4(2) — Processing**
"Processing" means any operation or set of operations performed on personal data, including
collection, recording, storage, adaptation, retrieval, disclosure, dissemination, or erasure.

Key point: Simply storing data in a database is "processing". Reading a log file is "processing".
Almost any interaction with personal data counts.

**Article 4(7) — Controller**
The "controller" is the natural or legal person which determines the purposes and means of the
processing. The controller is responsible for compliance.

**Article 4(11) — Consent**
"Consent" means any freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous indication of the data
subject's wishes by which they signify agreement to the processing of their personal data.
Pre-ticked boxes do not constitute valid consent. Silence is not consent.

## Article 5 — Principles of Processing

Article 5(1) sets out six core principles. All processing must comply with ALL of them.

**5(1)(a) — Lawfulness, fairness and transparency**
Personal data shall be processed lawfully, fairly and in a transparent manner.

**5(1)(b) — Purpose limitation**
Data shall be collected for specified, explicit and legitimate purposes. It must not be
processed in a manner incompatible with those purposes. You cannot collect data for one
purpose and re-use it for a different one without a new lawful basis.

**5(1)(c) — Data minimisation**
Data collected must be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary. You must not
collect more data than you actually need for the stated purpose.

**5(1)(d) — Accuracy**
Personal data must be accurate and kept up to date. Inaccurate data must be erased or rectified.

**5(1)(e) — Storage limitation**
Data must not be kept in a form that identifies individuals longer than necessary.
Indefinite retention of personal data violates this principle.

**5(1)(f) — Integrity and confidentiality**
Data must be processed securely, including protection against unauthorised access and accidental loss.

**5(2) — Accountability**
The controller is responsible for compliance with Article 5(1) and must be able to demonstrate it.

## Article 6 — Lawful Bases for Processing

Processing is only lawful if at least one of the following bases applies:

**6(1)(a) — Consent**
The data subject has given consent for one or more specific purposes.
Consent must be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous (see Article 4(11)).
Consent can be withdrawn at any time.

**6(1)(b) — Contractual necessity**
Processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party,
or to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract.

**6(1)(c) — Legal obligation**
Processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject.

**6(1)(d) — Vital interests**
Processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another person.

**6(1)(e) — Public task**
Processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest.

**6(1)(f) — Legitimate interests**
Processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller,
except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights of the data
subject. This basis requires a balancing test and cannot be used to override individuals' rights.

**Important**: Legitimate interests cannot be used by public authorities in the performance of
their tasks. Consent is required for processing children's data in online services.

## Article 7 — Conditions for Consent

Where processing is based on consent:

- The controller must be able to demonstrate that the data subject consented.
- If consent is given in a written declaration with other matters, the request must be
  presented in a manner clearly distinguishable from the other matters.
- The data subject has the right to withdraw consent at any time. Withdrawal must be as
  easy as giving consent.
- Processing before withdrawal of consent remains lawful.

## Article 13 & 14 — Transparency and Information Obligations

When personal data is collected, the controller must provide the data subject with:
- Identity and contact details of the controller
- Purposes and legal basis for processing
- Legitimate interests pursued (if Article 6(1)(f) applies)
- Recipients or categories of recipients
- Retention period
- Rights of the data subject (access, rectification, erasure, objection)
- Right to withdraw consent at any time

This information must be provided at the time of collection, in a concise, transparent,
intelligible and easily accessible form, using clear and plain language.

## Article 17 — Right to Erasure ("Right to be Forgotten")

The data subject has the right to obtain erasure of personal data without undue delay when:
- Data is no longer necessary for the purposes for which it was collected.
- The data subject withdraws consent and there is no other lawful basis.
- The data subject objects to processing and there are no overriding legitimate grounds.
- Data has been unlawfully processed.
- Data must be erased to comply with a legal obligation.

The controller must take reasonable steps to inform other controllers that the data subject
has requested erasure of any links to, or copies of, the personal data.

## Article 25 — Data Protection by Design and by Default

**By Design**: The controller must implement appropriate technical and organisational measures
designed to implement data protection principles (e.g. data minimisation) effectively.

**By Default**: The controller must ensure that, by default, only personal data which are
necessary for each specific purpose are processed. This applies to the amount of data collected,
extent of processing, storage periods, and accessibility.

Example: If a registration form asks for date of birth to verify age, it should not store the
exact date of birth permanently after verification — only whether the user is over the required age.

---

## Article 32 — Security of Processing

The controller and processor must implement technical and organisational measures to ensure
a level of security appropriate to the risk. This includes:

- Pseudonymisation and encryption of personal data
- Ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability of processing systems
- Ability to restore availability of personal data in the event of an incident
- Regular testing and evaluation of security measures

Risk assessment must consider the risks presented by the type of processing — in particular,
from accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorised disclosure.

## Article 83 — Administrative Fines

**Article 83(4)**: Infringements of basic obligations (Articles 8, 11, 25-39, 42-43) are
subject to fines up to €10,000,000 or 2% of total worldwide annual turnover.

**Article 83(5)**: Infringements of the basic principles (Articles 5, 6, 7, 9), rights of
data subjects (Articles 12-22), and international transfers are subject to fines up to
€20,000,000 or 4% of total worldwide annual turnover — whichever is higher.

The supervisory authority considers whether the infringement was negligent or intentional,
the categories of personal data involved, the degree of cooperation with the authority,
and how the controller became aware of the breach.

## Recital 26 — Anonymised Data

The principles of data protection do not apply to anonymous information — information which
does not relate to an identified or identifiable natural person, or to personal data rendered
anonymous in such a manner that the data subject is not or no longer identifiable.

Key point: If data is truly anonymised (not just pseudonymised), GDPR does not apply.
Pseudonymised data (where re-identification is possible with additional information) IS
still personal data and IS subject to GDPR.

## Recital 30 — Online Identifiers

Natural persons may be associated with online identifiers provided by their devices,
applications, tools and protocols, such as internet protocol addresses, cookie identifiers
or other identifiers such as radio frequency identification tags. This may leave traces which,
in particular when combined with unique identifiers and other information received by the servers,
may be used to create profiles of the natural persons and identify them.

Key point: IP addresses are explicitly identified as personal data in Recital 30 because they
can be used to identify a person when combined with other information held by the controller.
